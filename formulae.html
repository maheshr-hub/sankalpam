Formulae & References

How each panchāṅga element is defined and computed

A note on method
  • The definitions below follow the mūla-grantha astronomical texts — principally the Sūrya Siddhānta and, where relevant, the Āryabhaṭīya — for the core panchāṅga elements.
  • Celestial longitudes are currently computed using simplified, low-precision periodic-term formulas (in the tradition of Jean Meeus's Astronomical Algorithms), not a full ephemeris. Migrating this to Swiss Ephemeris is planned for a future update; this note will be revised once that's done.
  • This app cannot account for regional sampradāya variation. The exact panchāṅga followed by different traditions and regions — and your own family priest — may differ slightly from what's shown here, particularly around tithi/nakṣatra boundary days.

Saṃvatsara general reference

the named year within the 60-year Bṛhaspati (Jupiter) cycle
Formula
saṃvatsara index = (elapsed years since cycle epoch) mod 60 → name looked up from the fixed 60-name cycle (Prabhava … Akṣaya)
Reference

The 60-year Bṛhaspati cycle is described across the astronomical siddhāntas, and a shorter 5-year yuga cycle appears already in the Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa (c. 1st millennium BCE), the oldest surviving Indian astronomical text. The exact list of 60 names and its correlation to specific Gregorian years is a later systematization found in Purāṇic and Siddhāntic literature rather than one single canonical source, so this reference is kept general rather than pinned to one verse.

What it means

Each year is given one of 60 names, cycling continuously — used mainly to name the year in a sankalpa, not for any astronomical calculation of the year's other elements.

Ayana ancient concept, modern boundary

the sun's northward or southward half of the year
Formula
Uttarāyaṇa if sidereal solar longitude ∈ [270°, 360°) ∪ [0°, 90°) (Makara → Mithuna) Dakṣiṇāyana if sidereal solar longitude ∈ [90°, 270°) (Karka → Dhanu)
Reference

Ayana as a solstice-based division of the year is very old — referenced in the Ṛgveda and formalized calendrically in the Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa. Important caveat: because this app uses the sidereal (nirayana) zodiac, the "Uttarāyaṇa" boundary here is fixed to the Makara saṅkrānti point, not the true tropical winter solstice. Precession has moved these roughly 23 days apart from each other since antiquity — worth knowing if you compare against a tropical/Western solstice date.

What it means

Uttarāyaṇa (the sun's "northward course") is traditionally considered the more auspicious half of the year for major undertakings; Dakṣiṇāyana the "southward course."

Ṛtu ancient / Vedic

one of six two-month seasons
Formula
ṛtu index = floor(māsa index ÷ 2) → 6 seasons of 2 solar months each (Vasanta, Grīṣma, Varṣā, Śarad, Hemanta, Śiśira)
Reference

The six-season year is attested from the earliest Vedic literature (Ṛgveda, Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa) and is formally correlated to the solar calendar in the Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa — this is a classificatory grouping of months rather than a computed astronomical quantity in its own right.

What it means

Simply pairs consecutive solar months into a named season; carries no separate calculation beyond knowing the current māsa.

Māsa well-established

the solar month — this app uses the Tamil solar (rāśi-based) calendar
Formula
māsa index = floor(sidereal solar longitude ÷ 30°) → 0=Mēṣa (Chittirai) … 11=Mīna (Panguni)
Reference

Solar months defined by the sun's transit through each of the 12 rāśis are set out in the Sūrya Siddhānta's Madhyamādhikāra alongside its treatment of the sidereal zodiac. Note this app's daily Sankalpam uses this solar month throughout, distinct from the amānta lunar month (used elsewhere, e.g. for festivals like Rāma Navamī) which is defined by successive new moons rather than solar transits — the two systems only share month boundaries by coincidence in most years.

What it means

Marks a new month at each saṅkrānti (the sun's entry into a new rāśi), roughly every 30 days.

Pakṣa ancient / Vedic

the waxing or waning lunar fortnight
Formula
Śukla Pakṣa (waxing) if elongation < 180° Kṛṣṇa Pakṣa (waning) if elongation ≥ 180° where elongation = (moon longitude − sun longitude) mod 360°
Reference

The two fortnights are among the oldest calendrical concepts in the tradition, referenced already in the Ṛgveda and Atharvaveda, well before the astronomical siddhāntas gave them a precise degree-based boundary.

What it means

Śukla Pakṣa runs from new moon to full moon (waxing), Kṛṣṇa Pakṣa from full moon to new moon (waning) — 15 tithis each.

Tithi well-established

the lunar day — 1/30th of a synodic month
Formula
elongation = (moon longitude − sun longitude) mod 360° tithi index = floor(elongation ÷ 12°) → 0..29, naming resets each pakṣa
Reference

This is a directly classical definition. The Sūrya Siddhānta's Madhyamādhikāra defines the lunar day as the time taken for the moon to gain 12° of elongation on the sun (see Ebenezer Burgess's 1860 public-domain English translation, published by the American Oriental Society); the same underlying idea — dividing the synodic month into 30 equal elongation-based units — is echoed in the Āryabhaṭīya's Kālakriyāpāda.

(paraphrase, not a literal verse quotation) — a tithi is complete when the moon has gained twelve degrees of elongation over the sun, reckoned from their last conjunction (amāvāsyā).
What it means

30 tithis make one synodic (lunar) month: 15 waxing (Śukla) + 15 waning (Kṛṣṇa). Unlike a solar day, a tithi's length varies (roughly 19–26 hours) because the moon's angular speed isn't constant.

Vāsara origin debated

the weekday, ruled by one of the seven grahas
Formula
vāsara index = (Julian Day Number + 1) mod 7 → cycles through Ravi, Soma, Maṅgala, Budha, Guru, Śukra, Śani
Reference

Flagged deliberately: the seven-day week with planetary rulership is not found in the earliest Vedic or Siddhāntic strata. Historians of Indian astronomy (notably David Pingree) date its clear appearance in India to around the 5th century CE — the earliest known epigraphic attestation is the Eraṇ inscription (485 CE). Whether it developed independently or through contact with Hellenistic/Babylonian astronomy that used the same "Chaldean order" of planets is genuinely debated among scholars, so no single classical text is cited here as the origin.

What it means

Each day is ruled by a graha in a fixed repeating order — the same order used for the hour-rulers (horā) from which the day-ruler sequence is traditionally derived.

Nakṣatra ancient concept, later formula

the lunar mansion the moon occupies
Formula
nakṣatra index = floor(moon longitude ÷ 13°20′) → 0..26
Reference

The 27 nakṣatras themselves are far older than any astronomical siddhānta — the list appears already in the Atharvaveda and the Taittirīya Brāhmaṇa, and the Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa uses them to structure its calendar. The neat mathematical division of the ecliptic into 27 equal 13°20′ segments is the later formalization found in the Sūrya Siddhānta's Madhyamādhikāra, once a precise sidereal zodiac was in use.

What it means

Marks which of the 27 (occasionally 28, with Abhijit) lunar mansions the moon is transiting — used for the nakṣatra of the day and for auspicious-timing calculations generally.

Yoga origin uncertain

a combined sun-moon angular measure, one of 27
Formula
yoga index = floor((sun longitude + moon longitude) ÷ 13°20′) → 0..26
Reference

Flagged deliberately: unlike tithi and nakṣatra, no single siddhānta text explicitly sets out this construction, and its astronomical rationale is not obviously meaningful (unlike tithi, which directly reflects the moon's phase). The historian S. B. Dikshit remarked in his Bhāratīya Jyotiṣ Śāstra that "it is not known what planetary position in the sky is indicated by yoga, and it is useful only in astrology." One plausible line of development: the related concepts of vyatīpāta and vaidhṛti — moments when the sun+moon longitude sum reaches a half or full circle — do appear in the Brahma-sphuṭa-siddhānta of Brahmagupta, and the 27-yoga scheme may have generalized from there as a natural counterpart to tithi's subtraction with an addition.

What it means

Used mainly for astrological/muhūrta purposes rather than calendrical ones; carries no lunar-phase meaning the way tithi does.

Karaṇa well-established

half a tithi
Formula
karaṇa index = floor(elongation ÷ 6°) → 0..59 per month naming cycles through 7 movable karaṇas (repeating) + 4 fixed karaṇas (once each, at month boundaries)
Reference

Karaṇa is a direct corollary of the tithi definition — the same Sūrya Siddhānta passage that defines the 12° tithi unit underlies the 6° half-unit. Its treatment as a distinct element of the five-limbed (pañca-aṅga) panchāṅga is consistent across the classical astronomical tradition.

What it means

Each tithi contains exactly two karaṇas. 60 karaṇas occur per synodic month, but only 11 names are used: 7 "movable" (chara) names repeat 8 times each across the month, while 4 "fixed" (sthira) names each occur exactly once, always at the same point in the lunar month.

Citations aim to be precise where the textual basis is well-established, and clearly flagged where genuinely uncertain or debated among historians of Indian astronomy — rather than forcing a confident-sounding reference where none exists. Corrections welcome.
